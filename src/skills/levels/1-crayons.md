# Crayons

## Who you're writing for

Someone with no science background at all, or a bright ten-year-old. They want to know what the
scientists were trying to find out and whether it worked. They will stop reading the moment a
sentence needs a dictionary.

## Language

- Short sentences. One idea per sentence, one idea per post.
- Everyday words only. If the paper's subject needs a name ("neutrino"), give it once with a
  plain description ("a tiny particle that passes through almost anything") and then use it.
- No other technical terms. "Detector", "beam" and "energy" are fine; "appearance probability",
  "baseline" and "σ" are not.
- Warm and plain, never cutesy. No "Imagine…!", no talking down.

## Numbers and maths

- No equations, symbols or units the reader would not use at home.
- At most two numbers in the whole thread, rounded and made concrete ("three times as dense",
  "a few thousand", "about the distance from London to Rome").
- Turning a number into an everyday comparison is where mistakes creep in. Work the arithmetic
  out from the paper's own values (1 MeV is about a thousandth of a proton's mass, not a
  millionth); if a comparison needs a conversion you are unsure of, drop the number instead.

## Comparisons

The one level where comparisons are encouraged: one per thread, drawn from everyday life, and
only if it is true to how the thing works. Say plainly that it is a comparison. Never stack them.

## Shape

Hook under about 240 characters. 3-5 posts after the hook, never more: leave detail out rather than add posts. Each section can be a single post; the caveat can be one sentence.

## What to dwell on

What question the scientists asked, why anyone cares, and whether the answer was yes, no or "not
yet". Skip how the method works beyond one sentence.

## Avoid

- Making the result sound bigger than it is. "Not yet" and "it turned out not to work" are good
  endings.
- Hiding uncertainty. Say "the scientists think" or "their calculations suggest" when that is
  what happened.

## Calibration

**Headline:** A giant underground particle beam could, in theory, sense where earthquakes form,
but far too slowly to warn anyone

**Hook:** Scientists asked whether tiny particles shot through the ground could notice the
extra-dense rock near a fault before an earthquake. They can, a little, but you would need years
of measurements to see it, and a warning has to come in days.
