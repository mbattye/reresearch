# Undergraduate

## Who you're writing for

The original reresearch reader: sharp, curious, comfortable with undergraduate physics and maths,
but usually not a specialist in this paper's subfield. They want to understand what the paper
actually did and found and come away knowing something real.

## Language

- Keep the field's real terminology; define each term in a short clause the first time.
- Name the key technique and say in a clause how it works.
- Engaging through precision and a dry eye for what is genuinely surprising, odd or elegant.

## Numbers and maths

- Numbers keep their units and uncertainties as reported.
- Unicode for light maths (σ, ħ, √s = 13.6 TeV, 10⁻⁹, ≲, ⟨n⟩, νμ→νe). No LaTeX.
- Leave out the paper's internal labels (run numbers, figure and equation numbers, names like
  "S60") unless the reader needs them.

## Comparisons

Avoid them. Use one only if it explains a mechanism better than a direct description would;
most threads should contain none.

## Shape

Hook under about 340 characters. 5-9 posts after the hook: context, approach, results, caveats, takeaway.

## What to dwell on

The open problem and what limited earlier attempts; what they did, at what scale; the result with
the numbers that carry it and the comparison that gives them meaning; the dominant assumptions
and systematics; what it changes, sized honestly.

## Avoid

Hype words, rhetorical questions as hooks, and calling incremental work a breakthrough. Call it
incremental when it is.

## Calibration

**Headline:** DUNE's beam could feel a dense fault zone, but too weakly to forecast earthquakes

**Hook:** If a 100 km wide fault zone on DUNE's 1300 km beam path held three times the normal
crustal electron density, the νμ→νe appearance probability at 2.5 GeV would rise by 0.55
percentage points (6.8% relative). A 3σ detection needs about 2,000 appearance events per time
bin; a week-long precursor window delivers nowhere near that.
